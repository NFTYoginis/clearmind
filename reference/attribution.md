# Reference — Attribution

> ClearMind's backstage machinery draws on well-established thinking tools. We credit their originators plainly and in our own words. We never claim these methods as our own invention, and we never present them to the person mid-session (the machinery stays invisible — see `rules.md`). This file is for the README, for an honest answer if a person asks "where does this come from," and for the team.

What ClearMind contributes is **not** a new framework. It is the *sequencing and concealment* — reading a person's signals, summoning the right tool at the right moment, keeping all of it backstage, and ending in a written crossing. The tools themselves are borrowed and credited.

---

## The coaching questions (the fog movement)

The opening movement — staying with the person, asking what's on their mind, asking *what else,* asking them to say more, getting under the apparent problem to the real one before reaching for any solution — is grounded in the **coaching-question tradition**, most directly **Michael Bungay Stanier**'s work in *The Coaching Habit* (and related writing). His insight that the *first* problem named is rarely the *real* one, and that staying curious one beat longer beats jumping to advice, is the backbone of how ClearMind handles fog.

We adapt it: ClearMind uses these as a felt conversation, never as a labeled questionnaire.

## The desires / gratitude / loves priming (the orientation movement)

The orientation movement — gently surfacing what the person *wants,* what they're *grateful* for, and what they *love* — is a **priming / nervous-system-settling practice** of the kind common in journaling and coaching work (sometimes run as "ten wants, ten gratitudes, ten loves"). We use it not as a manifestation gimmick but for two concrete jobs: it settles a still-loaded nervous system enough that the person can think, and it brings their actual desires into the light so they can feed the next movement's outcomes. We do not claim a single definitive originator for this priming practice and don't attribute it to one; it is a widely-used pattern we apply for a specific purpose.

## The decision-structuring method (the formation movement)

The formation movement — laying out the **O**utcomes you want, the **O**ptions available, the **C**onsequences of each, then **E**valuating, **M**itigating the risks, and **R**esolving — is **Tony Robbins**'s **OOCEMR** decision-making framework, credited to him. We use its structure to help a person see the *real* fork clearly, with the wants surfaced in orientation feeding the Outcomes step. The person never hears the acronym.

## The crossing (the commitment movement)

The closing move — converting clarity into a concrete, owned commitment with a next step and a plan for the obstacle — reflects a broad behavior-change principle that **people act on commitments, not on analyses.** This is widely supported across decision and behavior-change literature; ClearMind's specific contribution is making the commitment *visible and savable* as the crossing document.

## How to credit if asked

If a person asks where ClearMind comes from, answer plainly and briefly, then return to them:

> *"It leans on a few well-worn thinking tools — a coaching style of questioning (Michael Bungay Stanier's work is a touchstone), a decision-structuring method from Tony Robbins, and some everyday priming and commitment practices. I just don't run them at you by name, because the point is the conversation, not the scaffolding."*

In public-facing materials, credit lives in the README and here — not as badges on the live tool.
